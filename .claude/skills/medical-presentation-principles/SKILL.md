# Medical Presentation Principles

Source principles: [many-ppt-skills/principles](https://github.com/brycewang-stanford/many-ppt-skills/tree/main/principles). The original eight documents are included in `references/` under CC BY 4.0. See `LICENSE-CC-BY-4.0.txt`.

Use this skill when creating or substantially revising a medical presentation, lecture deck, poster-style slide set, or visual teaching artifact. Apply the eight source principles with the medical-specific priorities below.

## Medical priorities

1. Scientific accuracy and source traceability come before visual novelty. Do not invent clinical facts, doses, thresholds, citations, or patient details. Verify current clinical recommendations when presenting them as current; put compact citations near the relevant claims and a reference slide at the end.
2. Preserve clinically important nuance. Distinguish screening from diagnosis, initial from confirmatory tests, stabilization from definitive treatment, and guideline recommendations from local practice when relevant. Never omit a red flag or safety-critical caveat just to reduce slide density.
3. Default to clear Arabic with standard English medical terms in parentheses when the audience is Arabic-speaking medical students. Follow the user's requested language and level if specified.
4. Teach through the content: use a clinical case, labeled anatomy, annotated imaging, flowchart, or comparison when it explains the mechanism or decision. Avoid decorative medical icons and stock imagery that do not carry information. Label images, axes, units, laterality, and key structures.
5. Control information density without discarding examinable or safety-critical content. Use progressive disclosure across slides, a clear takeaway per slide where suitable, and readable labels. Move detail to speaker notes or backup slides only when that preserves the requested coverage.
6. Keep a consistent visual system: deliberate palette, type hierarchy, spacing, and a small set of layouts. Use color coding only when it has a stable meaning, and include text/shape cues so meaning does not depend on color alone. Treat banned design patterns as context-sensitive checks, not universal law; institutional templates and accessibility take precedence.
7. Treat slides as a fixed presentation canvas. Use the required aspect ratio (usually 16:9) and preserve it when exporting. Do not make slide content reflow like a webpage. If the requested deliverable is web-based, follow its actual target instead.
8. When the visual direction is unclear, make a few genuinely different sample slides using the user's real topic and content, then refine from their response. Skip this when the brief or template already specifies the direction.
9. Inspect the rendered deck when practical. Check clipping, overlap, legibility at presentation size, consistent terminology and units, figure labels, citation visibility, and that no drafting notes or template labels remain. For a PowerPoint, verify the exported PDF or slide images; for HTML, inspect the rendered slides.
10. Keep the skill and output focused. Read only the relevant source principles from `references/` as needed. Do not force a fixed narrative template when the clinical material calls for another order.

## Workflow

- Establish audience, learning objective, scope, language, slide count/time, required source material, and delivery format from the request and available files.
- Build and verify the clinical argument before styling. Separate supplied-source content from any external clarification when requested.
- Choose a restrained visual system and slide sequence that supports the clinical reasoning.
- Produce the requested artifact, then render and review it when practical. Report what was actually checked.
